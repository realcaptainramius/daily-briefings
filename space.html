Blue Origin

Nov 12–16Blue Origin puts Orbital Reef on hold and will not bid in NASA’s next commercial station round

GeekWire and others report that CEO Dave Limp told staff at a Nov 12 all-hands meeting that Orbital Reef will not enter NASA’s next commercial LEO destinations competition, and that Blue Origin will not seek more NASA funding for the station. The company said it is focusing resources on lunar capabilities tied to Artemis, including its Blue Moon lander work. Coverage describes the project as on hold, not formally cancelled. Partner Sierra Space has not publicly detailed its own plans. Reports disagree on the bid deadline (Dec 8 in one summary, Dec 14 in others). Seen via search summaries only; Blue Origin’s statement was not reviewed directly.

Nov 10Blue Origin says redesigned BE-4 oxygen valve is qualified; GS1-3 investigation to close this month

Blue Origin’s Nov 10 “GS1-3 Hotfire Updates” post concerns the May 28 New Glenn static-fire explosion at LC-36 that destroyed booster GS1-3. Per a search summary of the post, the root cause was traced to the main oxygen valve on one BE-4 engine; a redesigned valve has passed qualification testing, is installed in engines for upcoming flights and is being rolled out fleetwide, and the company expects to close the investigation later this month. The post itself could not be fetched. Around the same time Blue Origin reportedly announced the Northstar LOX/LNG booster engine and a GS3 third stage, and asked the FAA to raise New Glenn’s licensed cadence to 50 launches a year; search summaries date the GS3 and Northstar unveiling to Nov 5, and the FAA cadence request has no confirmed date. No closure of the investigation had been announced as of Nov 16.

SpaceX

Nov 13–15Starship Flight 15: ship caught by Starbase tower for the first time; booster catch aborted, lost in Gulf

The FAA issued the license modification for Flight 15, covering return of both stages, on Nov 13, reportedly only hours before launch. Booster 22 and Ship 42 lifted off from Starbase Pad 2 at about 5:47 p.m. Eastern on Nov 13. The booster’s tower catch was called off: SpaceX first cited a guidance (GNC) abort on the webcast, later said GNC worked as intended, and pointed to a low-fuel-mass alarm that cut the boostback burn short. The booster diverted to the Gulf of Mexico, where reports say its flight termination system destroyed it just above the water. Musk is quoted by secondary outlets blaming slight engine underperformance, and Space.com says SpaceX is still investigating. Ship 42 deployed all 26 Starlink V3 satellites, made 14 orbits and was caught by the tower arms about 22.5 hours after launch on Nov 14 (reported as about 4:20 p.m. Eastern; one summary gave 3:21 p.m.). It was the first Starship upper stage recovered this way and the longest Starship flight to date. After inspecting Ship 42 at Starbase on Sunday, Nov 15, Musk said it “could probably be reflown with a few weeks of refurbishment” but that “many small fixes” are still needed for airplane-like reuse; this is Musk’s own estimate, SpaceX has never reflown a ship and has set no reflight date. SpaceX has not announced a date for Flight 16. No FAA mishap determination for the booster loss had been reported as of Nov 17. Seen via search summaries only.

Nov 12–16Ukraine reports new Starshield speed limits; WSJ says Musk ordered them, SpaceX denies; senators demand records

Ukraine said on Nov 12 that SpaceX’s Starshield service again faced restrictions, an effective cap for terminals moving faster than about 100 mph (161 km/h) that hampers strike and interceptor drones. This follows an Oct 13–24 disruption. The Wall Street Journal reported that Musk ordered the limits after talks with President Trump. SpaceX denied imposing restrictions, a Nov 15 Ukrainian report says SpaceX attributed slowdowns to “technical issues,” and Trump denied the White House ordered them. Per WSJ-sourced reports from Nov 13–14, Democratic Sens. Jeanne Shaheen and Chris Coons asked Musk to preserve records and answer by Nov 30. The core claims are contested and rest on anonymous sourcing. Seen via search summaries only.

Axiom Space

Nov 13Axiom completes NASA critical design review for AxEMU spacesuit

Axiom said on Nov 13 that it has completed NASA’s Critical Design Review for the AxEMU suit, including system-level checks such as whole-suit mass properties and pressure-drop performance. Next comes production: finishing the pressure garment and assembling the portable life support system by early 2027, then qualification testing (thermal vacuum, vibration, life-cycle) before certification. Completing CDR does not mean the suit is flight-certified. This follows reports that NASA is having Axiom build a lighter “sortie suit” for the first Artemis landing. Seen via search summaries of the release.

Axiom Space (Nov 13) · The Defense News
Nov 10Axiom signs MOU with Nitride Global on in-space semiconductor research; False Claims Act suit against Vast unsealed

Axiom announced an MOU with Nitride Global to research manufacturing ultrawide-bandgap semiconductor materials in orbit, building on a NASA SBIR project on aluminum nitride crystal growth. Separately, reports dated Nov 10 say a False Claims Act suit Axiom filed under seal in Houston federal court on June 19 against Vast Space, alleging Vast’s NASA mission-planning document used Axiom proprietary information, was unsealed after a Nov 4 order; the allegations are unproven and Vast’s response was not seen. Final proposals in NASA’s commercial station competition are reportedly due in mid-December (reports cite Dec 8 or Dec 14). Seen via search summaries.
