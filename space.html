Firefly Aerospace

Nov 17Firefly adds QASCOM as commercial customer for Elytra lunar-orbit navigation payload

Firefly announced on Nov 17 that it will host QASCOM’s COLLINS payload, developed for the Italian Space Agency, on its Elytra Dark orbital vehicle in lunar orbit. COLLINS is meant to demonstrate spacecraft navigation using GNSS signals and future LunaNet positioning, navigation and timing services, following the LuGRE receiver that flew on Blue Ghost Mission 1, but this time under a commercial contract with Firefly. Elytra is to support six months of COLLINS operations, with an option to extend. Reports differ on which mission and year it flies (one secondary outlet says a 2028 lunar mission, an Italian report says Blue Ghost Mission 3), and no contract value was reported. Seen via search summaries of the release.

Blue Origin

Nov 15–18ULA Vulcan carrying 40 Amazon Leo satellites fails; preliminary data point to fuel supply, not Blue Origin’s BE-4 engines

United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan, powered by two Blue Origin BE-4 engines, failed about 10 minutes into flight on Sunday, Nov 15, losing 40 Amazon Leo satellites; it is ULA’s first outright mission failure. ULA first said early data pointed to first-stage underperformance, and on Nov 18 said preliminary indications are that the low booster thrust resulted from an inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to the BE-4 engines, not from the engines themselves, which appear to have operated as planned. The finding is preliminary, the investigation with the FAA is ongoing, and Vulcan remains grounded. Before the flight, ULA said the engines had been retrofitted with Blue Origin’s fix for the oxygen-valve problem behind May’s New Glenn explosion. Blue Origin says it is supporting ULA’s investigation, and CEO Dave Limp reportedly said updates should come from ULA. No change to New Glenn’s January 2027 return-to-flight target has been reported. Seen via search summaries only. One outlet’s headline claiming ULA attributed the failure to the BE-4s conflicts with ULA’s Nov 18 statement.

Nov 12–16Blue Origin puts Orbital Reef on hold and will not bid in NASA’s next commercial station round

GeekWire and others report that CEO Dave Limp told staff at a Nov 12 all-hands meeting that Orbital Reef will not enter NASA’s next commercial LEO destinations competition, and that Blue Origin will not seek more NASA funding for the station. Partner Sierra Space has not publicly detailed its own plans. Reports disagree on the bid deadline (Dec 8 in one summary, Dec 14 in others). SpaceX

Nov 13–15Starship Flight 15: ship caught by Starbase tower for the first time; booster catch aborted, lost in Gulf

The FAA issued the license modification for Flight 15, covering return of both stages, on Nov 13, reportedly only hours before launch. Booster 22 and Ship 42 lifted off from Starbase Pad 2 at about 5:47 p.m. Eastern on Nov 13. The booster’s tower catch was called off: SpaceX first cited a guidance (GNC) abort on the webcast, later said GNC worked as intended, and pointed to a low-fuel-mass alarm that cut the boostback burn short. The booster diverted to the Gulf of Mexico, where reports say its flight termination system destroyed it just above the water. Musk is quoted by secondary outlets blaming slight engine underperformance, and Space.com says SpaceX is still investigating. Ship 42 deployed all 26 Starlink V3 satellites, made 14 orbits and was caught by the tower arms about 22.5 hours after launch on Nov 14 (reported as about 4:20 p.m. Eastern; one summary gave 3:21 p.m.). It was the first Starship upper stage recovered this way and the longest Starship flight to date. After inspecting Ship 42 at Starbase on Sunday, Nov 15, Musk said it “could probably be reflown with a few weeks of refurbishment” but that “many small fixes” are still needed for airplane-like reuse; this is Musk’s own estimate, SpaceX has never reflown a ship and has set no reflight date. SpaceX has not announced a date for Flight 16. No FAA mishap determination for the booster loss had been reported as of Nov 19. Seen via search summaries only.

Nov 17–18SpaceX’s $40B off-balance-sheet AI-chip financing reportedly heads to bond buyers

Bloomberg reported on Nov 17 that roughly $4 billion of bonds from the approximately $40 billion debt package arranged by Goldman Sachs and Apollo to fund Nvidia Rubin GPUs for SpaceX could be sold as soon as this week. Per secondary summaries, the debt sits in a special-purpose vehicle managed by Apollo-backed Atlas SP that buys the chips and leases them to SpaceX for five years, keeping the debt off SpaceX’s balance sheet; it includes an $8.3 billion syndicated loan, and the notes are expected to be rated A3 (Moody’s) and A- (Kroll). Terms may change while the deal is marketed, and as of Nov 18 coverage no pricing or final terms had been reported. Seen via search summaries; the Bloomberg article itself was not read.

Nov 17–18Cameron County commissioners decline to advance SpaceX tax break for $24B Starbase-area data center

The Cameron County Commissioners Court voted 3–2 on Nov 17 against scheduling a public hearing on SpaceX’s request for a 10-year, 100% property-tax abatement for a proposed $24 billion data center and gas-fired power plant near Starbase. That is a procedural vote, not a ruling on the abatement itself, but local coverage says it effectively ends the county-level request. SpaceX had warned it could move the investment elsewhere without the abatement. Per a Nov 18 local report, Texas environmental regulators issued SpaceX an air permit for the site the day before the vote, and a separate abatement request before the City of Starbase is reportedly set for a Dec 9 hearing. Separately, market-news outlets report CFO Bret Johnsen said at an Austin summit on Nov 17 that SpaceX could be “one of, if not the largest player in AI infrastructure” next year if it meets its targets; that is the company’s own projection. Seen via search summaries, mostly of local and market-news outlets.

Nov 12–19Falcon 9 USSF-317 slips again; now targeting the evening of Nov 19 Pacific

The classified Space Force mission USSF-317 from SLC-4E at Vandenberg, earlier targeted for Nov 12, 13, 14–15 and 18, did not launch in its 03:00–07:00 UTC Nov 19 window (Wednesday evening Pacific) and slipped again by a day; no reason was given. SpaceX is now targeting Thursday, Nov 19, with a four-hour window opening at 6:46 p.m. Pacific (02:46 UTC Nov 20), with a backup on Friday, Nov 20; SpaceX’s own mission page reportedly lists conflicting opening times. The booster, on its 11th flight, is expected to land on the droneship Of Course I Still Love You. One analysis suggests the payload may be classified LEO sensing satellites; that is unconfirmed. This is a target, not a confirmed launch, and the window opens after this edition was compiled. Seen via search summaries only.

Nov 12–18Ukraine reports new Starshield speed limits; WSJ says Musk ordered them, SpaceX denies; senators demand records

Ukraine said on Nov 12 that SpaceX’s Starshield service again faced restrictions, an effective cap for terminals moving faster than about 100 mph (161 km/h) that hampers strike and interceptor drones. This follows an Oct 13–24 disruption. The Wall Street Journal reported that Musk ordered the limits after talks with President Trump. SpaceX denied imposing restrictions, a Nov 15 Ukrainian report says SpaceX attributed slowdowns to “technical issues,” and Trump denied the White House ordered them. Per WSJ-sourced reports from Nov 13–14, Democratic Sens. Jeanne Shaheen and Chris Coons asked Musk to preserve records and answer by Nov 30. On Nov 17, President Zelensky said the Starshield speed limit “in the temporarily occupied territories remains in place” and that Ukraine is discussing it with the US side; no report of the limits being lifted had appeared as of Nov 18. The core claims are contested and rest on anonymous sourcing. Seen via search summaries only.

Axiom Space

Nov 13Axiom completes NASA critical design review for AxEMU spacesuit

Axiom said on Nov 13 that it has completed NASA’s Critical Design Review for the AxEMU suit, including system-level checks such as whole-suit mass properties and pressure-drop performance. Next comes production: finishing the pressure garment and assembling the portable life support system by early 2027, then qualification testing (thermal vacuum, vibration, life-cycle) before certification. Completing CDR does not mean the suit is flight-certified. This follows reports that NASA is having Axiom build a lighter “sortie suit” for the first Artemis landing. Seen via search summaries of the release.

Relativity Space

Nov 16Relativity’s October update: Terran R second stage completes first hot fire at Stennis

Relativity’s “October 2026 Company Update,” posted Nov 16, reports that Terran R’s second stage completed a 10-second hot fire on NASA Stennis’s A2 stand, which had not hosted a stage test since 1970; the test itself was in mid-October per the company’s social posts, and a mission-duty-cycle hot fire is next.
