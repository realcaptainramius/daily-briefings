Blue Origin

Nov 25EnduroSat books two dedicated New Glenn launches, first targeted for 2029

Bulgarian satellite maker EnduroSat announced a multi-launch agreement with Blue Origin for two dedicated New Glenn missions from Cape Canaveral, reportedly on the 7x2 configuration, carrying satellites EnduroSat builds for new and existing customers. The first launch is targeted for 2029; no contract value was reported in the coverage seen. It is New Glenn’s second announced booking in about a week, after Google’s Nov 19 Project Suncatcher deal. New Glenn has been grounded since May’s explosion and is targeting a return to flight in January 2027, so the 2029 date is a target. SpaceX

Nov 25FCC conditionally authorizes SpaceX’s Starmind orbital data-center constellation of up to one million satellites

The FCC’s Space Bureau, acting under delegated authority, conditionally authorized Starmind, SpaceX’s planned network of up to one million orbital data-center satellites applied for in January; coverage calls it the first such authorization in the US. The satellites would fly in six shells between roughly 500 and 1,002 km, but the debris plan was accepted only for satellites below 600 km, and four of the six shells sit above that line; SpaceX must file a modification with a final debris plan before deploying higher, and outlets differ on whether its request to use disposal “graveyard” orbits was deferred or denied. Per several reports, the grant lapses unless SpaceX has a working satellite by Nov 25, 2033, 100,000 by 2035, 500,000 by 2038 and the full million by 2040. Amazon, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society had petitioned to deny; the AAS said it was “disheartened and dismayed,” and the FCC said many feasibility concerns are outside its mandate. The FCC said it is still reviewing other orbital data-center applications. SpaceX shares (SPCX) fell as much as about 9% intraday before closing up about 1.3% at $127.44; one outlet’s report of a 7.5% fall to $116.32 appears to reflect an intraday level. Seen via search summaries only; the FCC order itself was not read.

Nov 24–25FT: SpaceX and Tesla name directors to lead possible merger talks; SpaceX shares fall about 5.7%

The Financial Times reported on Nov 24 that Tesla and SpaceX have each named board members to committees negotiating a possible all-stock merger: per the FT as relayed by others, Robyn Denholm, Kathleen Wilson-Thompson and Jack Hartung for Tesla, and Roelof Botha and Randy Glein for SpaceX, with Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs reportedly advising SpaceX and Evercore advising Tesla. People involved reportedly cautioned that the committees are not close to agreement. No deal has been announced, and any merger would need Tesla shareholder and regulatory approval. SpaceX shares (SPCX) fell about 5.7% to $125.81 that day as other space and AI stocks also fell, then fell further intraday on Nov 25 amid reported dilution worries before closing higher after the FCC Starmind approval (see above). No further announcement had been made as of Nov 26. Seen via search summaries of secondary coverage; the FT article was not read.

Nov 23Virginia and Oregon sue SpaceX over Grok-generated sexual images, including of children

Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones and Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield filed separate lawsuits under state consumer-protection laws on Monday, Nov 23. They allege that the Grok chatbot of SpaceX’s AI unit (formerly xAI) was launched without adequate safeguards, let users turn real photos into nude or sexual images, and that the company misled consumers about its protections. Virginia’s suit names SpaceX, X and the former xAI unit and cites a Center for Countering Digital Hate estimate that Grok generated millions of sexualized images between December 2025 and January 2026, more than 20,000 of them depicting children. Coverage describes these as the first state lawsuits after months of multistate demand letters. These are allegations only; SpaceX did not immediately comment. Seen via search summaries only; the complaints were not reviewed, and outlets differ on courts and relief sought.

Nov 22Falcon 9 launches NROL-136 spy-satellite mission 32 hours after USSF-317 from the same pad

A Falcon 9 launched NROL-136 for the National Reconnaissance Office from SLC-4E at Vandenberg at 4:23 a.m. Pacific (12:23 UTC) on Sunday, Nov 22, and the NRO said the mission succeeded. It was the 15th launch for the NRO’s proliferated reconnaissance architecture, built with SpaceX and Northrop Grumman; the payload’s details are classified. The booster, B1097 on its 14th flight per NASASpaceflight, landed at Landing Zone 4. Liftoff came about 32 hours after USSF-317 left the same pad, which NASASpaceflight says beat the SLC-4E turnaround record by about 23 hours and SpaceX’s fastest at any pad by about 13 hours. USSF-266 followed from the same pad on Nov 24 (see above). Seen via search summaries, including the NRO release.

Nov 20–21CRS-35 cargo Dragon leaves the ISS and splashes down off California

SpaceX’s CRS-35 cargo Dragon, launched Oct 13, undocked from the International Space Station at 12:05 p.m. Eastern on Friday, Nov 20, after poor weather delayed an earlier departure, and splashed down in the Pacific off California at 5:03 p.m. Eastern on Saturday, Nov 21, per Space.com. Reported return cargo includes a failed wrist joint from the station’s Canadarm2 robotic arm and frozen biological research samples. One outlet gives a conflicting splashdown date and time. Nov 12–20Falcon 9 launches classified USSF-317 mission for the Space Force after repeated delays

After targets of Nov 12, 13, 14–15, 18 and 19 and an unexplained scrub on Thursday evening, Nov 19, a Falcon 9 launched USSF-317 from SLC-4E at Vandenberg at 8:20 p.m. PST on Friday, Nov 20 (04:20 UTC Nov 21), and SpaceX said the payload reached orbit. Booster B1100, on its 11th flight, landed on the droneship Of Course I Still Love You about 8.5 minutes after liftoff. Spaceflight Now’s updated headline describes the payload as satellites for the Space Force’s data network constellation, but the number of spacecraft and their purpose remain classified; that observers think they may be Starshield satellites is speculation. It was reported as the sixth in a series of Space Force missions assigned to SpaceX since August. Seen via search summaries only.

Nov 17–25Apollo and Goldman price about $44B of notes for SpaceX’s leased Nvidia chips; shares dip below IPO price

Apollo Global Management and Goldman Sachs announced on Nov 20 that they had priced about $44 billion of senior and subordinated notes to finance Nvidia Vera Rubin GPUs and related equipment that an Apollo affiliate will buy and lease to SpaceX, under the roughly $40 billion lease SpaceX disclosed in October; coverage says the deal was oversubscribed and upsized. Nov 12–20Ukraine reports new Starshield speed limits; Musk calls Zelensky’s claims false; incoming House Intelligence chair threatens SpaceX funding

Ukraine said on Nov 12 that SpaceX’s Starshield service again faced restrictions, an effective cap for terminals moving faster than about 100 mph (161 km/h) that hampers strike and interceptor drones. This follows an Oct 13–24 disruption. The Wall Street Journal reported that Musk ordered the limits after talks with President Trump. SpaceX denied imposing restrictions, a Nov 15 Ukrainian report says SpaceX attributed slowdowns to “technical issues,” Trump denied the White House ordered them, and Musk has said limits are decided by the US military and “ultimately POTUS.” Per WSJ-sourced reports from Nov 13–14, Democratic Sens. Jeanne Shaheen and Chris Coons asked Musk to preserve records and answer by Nov 30. On Nov 17, President Zelensky said the speed limit “in the temporarily occupied territories remains in place”; at the DealBook Summit on Nov 18, Musk called the claims false and said Zelensky is “not always truthful,” per CNBC on Nov 19. CNN reported on Nov 20 that Rep. Jim Himes, who becomes House Intelligence Committee chair in January, told Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton in a classified message that the committee could “ring fence” funding for certain SpaceX projects in the annual Intelligence Authorization Act until it gets answers, and separately asked Musk whether the October limits were SpaceX’s own decision. That is a threat, not an enacted restriction, and it is unclear whether Himes has the votes. The core claims are contested and rest on anonymous sourcing; no report of the limits being lifted had appeared as of Nov 26. Seen via search summaries only.
